Election explainers
How elections work

Why a national vote is really 543 local contests

India’s first-past-the-post system turns constituency-level choices into a national parliament.

Read the short view

Each constituency elects one MP. The candidate with the most votes wins that seat, even without an absolute majority. That is why a party’s share of the national vote and its share of seats can differ—and why regional strength matters.

Coalitions

How alliances turn seat counts into a government

Parties negotiate not only over numbers, but also priorities, ministries and shared programmes.

Read the short view

A majority in the Lok Sabha is built from members who can support a government. Pre-poll and post-poll alliances can both matter; the public seat tally is a starting point for understanding the negotiations, not a full account of them.

Representation

What a constituency can—and cannot—tell us

Local results reflect local candidates and concerns as well as national campaigns.

Read the short view

Constituency outcomes combine many factors: candidate reputation, local issues, party organisation and broader political mood. A single seat is one part of a larger pattern; it should not be treated as a perfect proxy for every voter in a region.

Counting & trust

Where to find the authoritative result

Use official sources for final figures; independent trackers are best read as guides.

Read the short view

The Election Commission of India publishes official election schedules and constituency results. Check primary sources when a number is consequential, and note whether a report describes a lead during counting or a declared final result.
